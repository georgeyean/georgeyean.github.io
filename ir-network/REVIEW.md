# International Relations (general), c. 1977–2026: where it went, what settled, what is open

A narrative companion to `graph.json` and `network.html` in this folder. The graph has 218 work nodes, 17 theme nodes and 237 typed relations. Each theme section follows the same order: **what was claimed → what the evidence settled → what is contested → gaps and research opportunities**. Node ids in brackets (e.g. `[keohane1984]`) point to entries in `graph.json`.

**How the works were chosen.**
- **Your library:** 101 works come from `papers.json`, mostly your *Gov Io*, *Gov Ir* and *LIO* groups. Their claims are condensed from the repo's "Theory & evidence" analyses.
- **Shared works:** works that also appear in the IPE or security maps (Ruggie, Stone, Morrow, Zürn) are imported from them unchanged, so the three maps agree.
- **Top-cited articles:** 92 come from the 400 most-cited 2006–2026 articles in fifteen journals, filtered to general-IR topics: institutions, law, human rights, norms, legitimacy, regime complexity, order, opinion and climate. Their metadata comes from Crossref.
- **Anchors:** 25 are anchoring books and articles (Keohane, Moravcsik, Wendt, Keck & Sikkink, Simmons). The 21 new ones were all found in Crossref; the rest were already checked for the other maps.

One-line claims for non-library works are my summaries and were not re-read this session.

This map overlaps on purpose with the other two. Trade and finance institutions sit in the IPE map; war, alliances and terrorism sit in the security map. Here the focus is how cooperation, law, norms and order work across issue areas.

## 1. Cooperation theory and international regimes

**Claimed.** Regimes are principles, norms, rules and procedures around which expectations converge `[krasner1982a]`. They can outlive the power that built them `[krasner1982b]`, and they persist after hegemony because they lower transaction costs and make reputation matter `[keohane1984]`. That builds on complex interdependence `[keohanenye1977]` and reciprocity among egoists `[axelrod1981]`. Realists answered that institutions have little independent effect `[mearsheimer1994]`. Liberals answered that state preferences, not institutions, drive outcomes `[moravcsik1997]`.

**Settled.** Cooperation problems differ (collaboration, coordination, suasion), and institutional forms follow them `[martin1992]`. Every cooperation problem also combines bargaining over terms with enforcement, so a long shadow of the future cuts both ways `[fearon1998]`. Cooperation spreads through networks `[kinne2013]`, and procedural actors such as negotiation chairs hold real power `[tallberg2010]`.

**Contested.** Whether the regime literature's functionalism explains institutions that persist for political rather than economic reasons, like OPEC as a "rational myth" `[colgan2014]`.

**Gaps and open questions.**
- *Cooperation under rivalry.* Regime theory assumed shared gains among partners. How institutions work when members are strategic rivals (US–China in the WTO, WHO, ITU) is under-theorized.
- *Cooperation in new domains.* AI governance, space, cyber norms and pandemic preparedness are being built now. The `[martin1992]` and `[koremenos2001]` toolkits could be applied prospectively to predict their design.

## 2. Institutional design, legalization and delegation

**Claimed.** States design institutions rationally: membership, scope, centralization, control and flexibility follow the cooperation problem `[koremenos2001]`. Legalization varies in obligation, precision and delegation `[abbott2000]`, and soft law is a deliberate choice `[abbottsnidal2000]`. Principal-agent theory explains delegation and agent slack `[hawkins2006]`, `[nielson2003]`.

**Settled.** Delegation and pooling are distinct dimensions of authority `[hooghemarks2015]`. Informality is a design choice for flexibility `[vabulas2013]`. Flexibility clauses deepen commitment `[rosendorff2001]`, `[kucik2008]`. Monitoring design follows the incentives of victims `[dai2002]`, and compliance-system design decides outcomes `[mitchell1994]`.

**Contested.** Whether rational design has been tested against alternatives `[wendt2001]`. Whether trust, not safeguards, explains binding institutions `[rathbun2011]`. Whether informal autonomy matters more for performance than formal design `[lall2017]`. Whether bureaucracies exploit slack `[vaubel2006]`.

**Gaps and open questions.**
- *Design for exit.* Withdrawal clauses, sunset provisions and suspension rules are now salient (Section 9). Few design studies treat exit provisions as the dependent variable.
- *Measuring design from text.* Treaty and charter texts are machine-readable. LLM-coded design features across thousands of agreements would let the field test design theory at scale, as DESTA did for trade (see the IPE map).

## 3. Treaty commitment, compliance and selection

**Claimed.** Most states comply, and noncompliance reflects ambiguity and capacity, so management beats enforcement `[chayes1993]`. The reply: high compliance reflects shallow treaties, and deep cooperation needs enforcement `[downs1996]`.

**Settled.** Legal commitment raises reputational costs `[simmons2000]`. Selection is real `[vonstein2005]`, but treaties can screen and constrain at once `[simmonshopkins2005]`, and spatial models help separate the two `[lupu2013ajps]`. Institutionally assisted settlements are complied with more `[mitchellhensel2007]`. Commitments break when domestic coalitions change `[leeds2009]`.

**Contested.** How much individual traits of decision makers matter `[hafnerburton2014]`. Whether revisionist leaders weaken regimes by publicizing others' violations `[carnegiecarson2019jop]`, and how intelligence-protecting institutions shape disclosure `[carnegiecarson2019]`.

**Gaps.** Compliance research is still dominated by a few regimes (IMF Article VIII, human rights, laws of war `[morrow2007]`). Systematic compliance data on environmental, health and technology agreements are thin.

## 4. The human rights regime: treaties, shaming and accountability

**Claimed.** Transnational advocacy networks change states through the boomerang pattern `[keck1998]`, and norms are internalized through a spiral `[risse1999]`. The skeptical reply is that ratification is expressive and not associated with better practices `[hathaway2002]`.

**Settled.** Treaties work conditionally. They matter most in transitional and partially democratic states, through domestic mobilization `[simmons2009]`. They matter least in the most repressive states `[hbtsutsui2007]` and where courts are weak `[powellstaton2009]`, `[lupu2013io]`. Who commits depends on expected enforcement costs `[hathaway2007]`. Empty promises still empower civil society `[hbtsutsui2005]`. Shaming improves some rights but not physical integrity `[hb2008]`, works with local INGO presence `[murdiedavis2012]`, and reduces atrocity severity `[krain2012]`. Trials did not destabilize transitions `[sikkink2007]` and reduce repression `[kimsikkink2010]`. The ICC deters atrocity `[josimmons2016]`, and vulnerable states join it to commit to restraint `[simmonsdanner2010]`.

**Contested.** Whether practices have improved at all: the answer depends on how a changing standard of accountability is modeled `[fariss2014]` versus `[cingranelli2018]`. Whether shaming is politicized by alignment `[lebovicvoeten2006]`, `[termanbyun2022]`. Whether ICC involvement prolongs wars `[prorok2017]`. Capacity gaps `[cole2015]` and multiparty dictatorships' cheap ratification `[vreeland2008]` complicate treaty effects.

**Gaps and open questions.**
- *Backlash and closing civic space.* States crack down on NGOs `[chaudhry2022]`, and digital tools enable preemptive repression `[dragulupu2021]`. Whether the spiral model survives a world of exported digital authoritarianism is a central open question.
- *Indicators and rankings.* Global performance indicators exert social pressure `[kelleysimmons2015]`. Rival indicators produced by China and other states are now appearing, and nobody has studied their effects.

## 5. Norms, socialization and constructivism

**Claimed.** Anarchy is what states make of it `[wendt1992]`, `[wendt1999]`. Norms have a life cycle of emergence, cascade and internalization `[finnemore1998]`. Institutions socialize through persuasion and social influence `[johnston2001]`. IOs are bureaucracies with authority of their own, and with pathologies `[barnettfinnemore1999]`. Epistemic communities shape policy under uncertainty `[haas1992]`.

**Settled.** IGO membership is associated with converging interests `[bearce2007]` and converging human rights practices `[greenhill2010]`. Persuasion operated in specific negotiations, such as the ICC's `[deitelhoff2009]`. Gatekeepers decide which issues become campaigns `[carpenter2007]`, `[carpenter2011]`.

**Contested.** Who makes norms. Weaker states build regional rules to resist great powers `[acharya2011]`. Norms also diffuse from below through social hierarchies `[towns2012]`. Stigmatized states push back `[adlernissen2014]`. NGOs may extend rather than counter state power `[sending2006]`.

**Gaps.** Norm research documents cascades well but explains norm decay and reversal poorly (torture, refoulement, nuclear testing taboos). Measuring norm strength over time from diplomatic text is feasible with validated LLM coding.

## 6. Power, informal governance and bureaucracy in IOs

**Settled.** Powerful states exercise informal control alongside formal rules `[stone2008]`, `[stone2013]`. Staff anticipate the leading shareholder `[clark2021wb]`, and reforms reward its allies `[carnegie2023]`. Aid buys UN votes credibly from democracies `[carterstone2015]`. Accession is geopolitical `[daviswilf2017]`. Preferences can be measured from UN votes `[bailey2017]`.

**Contested.** IO autonomy. Bureaucracies have authority `[barnettfinnemore1999]` and slack `[vaubel2006]`, and informal autonomy drives performance `[lall2017]`, while transparency helps only when enforced `[honig2023]`. Autocratic powers run their own IFIs `[clarkdolan_cottiero]`.

**Gaps.** Informal governance has been studied mostly for US influence over Bretton Woods institutions. Chinese informal influence in the UN system (staffing, agenda setting, peer review) is the obvious next case.

## 7. Legitimacy, politicization and accountability

**Claimed.** Global governance exercises authority that generates legitimation problems and contestation `[zurn2018]`. Legitimacy (beliefs) differs from legitimation (processes) `[tallbergzurn2019]`. Multilateralism can enhance democracy `[keohane2009]` or erode representation `[gartzke2011]`.

**Settled.** Elites trust IOs more than citizens do `[dellmuth2022]`. Contestation grows with authority `[hooghe2019]`, but it matters only when politicized through domestic opportunity structures `[devries2021]`, `[wratil2023]`. IO approval informs domestic publics `[thompson2006]`, `[grieco2011]`, `[fang2008]`. Security Council legitimacy rests on procedure and performance `[bindheupel2015]` and on signaling restraint `[voeten2005]`. Civil society pressure drives accountability reforms `[lall2025]`.

**Contested.** Whether the current legitimacy crisis is new: rhetorical challenges peaked in the 1970s `[kentikelenisvoeten2021]`. Whether legitimacy beyond intergovernmental settings is achievable `[bernstein2011]`.

**Gaps.** Legitimacy research measures attitudes toward a few big IOs. Legitimacy among Global South governments and publics toward rival institutions (BRICS bank, SCO, AIIB) is barely measured.

## 8. Regime complexes, overlap and institutional change

**Claimed and settled.** Issue areas are governed by regime complexes that invite forum shopping and legal inconsistency `[raustiala2004]`, `[alter2009]`, as in climate `[keohanevictor2011]`. Dissatisfied states shift or create regimes `[morsekeohane2014]`. Competition can produce a division of labor `[gehringfaude2014]` through deference `[pratt2018]`. Overlapping IOs pool when their leading shareholders align `[clark2021pool]`, and outside options drive influence redistribution `[lipscy2015]`. States shape regime status quos with an eye to future bargains `[kennard2023]`.

**Contested.** Whether complexity means fragmentation (`[greenhilllupu2017]` finds little). How to describe complexity: punctuated equilibrium `[colgan2012]`, hybrid public-private complexes `[abbottfaude2021]`, or multidimensional cubes `[eswesterwinter2021]`. Historical institutionalism offers tools `[fioretos2011]`.

**Gaps.** The field lacks a dataset of regime complexes over time with institutional interactions coded. Without it, claims about competition, deference and fragmentation rest on a few cases.

## 9. IO life cycles: creation, death and withdrawal

**Settled.** Few new IGOs are created and growth shifts to transnational regulators `[abbott2016]`. IGOs die young and in crowded fields `[eilstrup2018]`. Many become zombies, depending on secretariat quality `[gray2018]`. States withdraw when preferences diverge and nationalism rises `[borzyskowski2019]`. Withdrawal damages the leaver's cooperation `[schmidt2025]` and sparks reform while spilling over into shared regimes `[schmidt2023]`.

**Gaps.** The 2017–2025 wave of US withdrawals and threatened withdrawals (Paris Agreement, WHO, UNESCO, the Human Rights Council) is a natural experiment, and its effects on other members' behavior are only starting to be studied.

## 10. Domestic politics and international commitments

**Claimed and settled.** Negotiations are two-level games `[putnam1988]`, building on the second image reversed `[gourevitch1978]`. Democracies sign agreements to signal to voters `[mmr2002]`. Issue linkage reshapes domestic coalitions `[davis2004]`. Legal rulings give leaders political cover `[alleehuth2006]`. Audiences react to broken agreements mainly when they lack policy views `[chaudoin2014]`, and governments accused of violations manage their image `[morsepratt2022]`. International constraints lower pledge fulfillment `[schneider2024]`. IO membership is a seal of approval to markets `[gray2009]`.

**Gap.** Most two-level research studies democracies. How autocratic domestic politics (elite coalitions, nationalism, propaganda) shapes international commitments is less developed.

## 11. IOs, democratization and election monitoring

**Settled.** Democratic regional IOs promote transitions `[pevehouse2002]`, democratizers join IOs to commit `[mansfield2006]`, and IO membership helps consolidation but cannot stop reversals `[poasturpelainen2015]`. Democracy diffuses regionally `[gleditschward2006]`. Election monitoring spread as a norm `[kelley2008]` and as a signal that even cheaters must send `[hyde2011]`.

**Contested.** Enforcement of democratic norms is selective `[donno2010]`. Autocrats game reputational signals, for example through gender quotas `[bushzetterberg2021]`, `[hughes2015]`. Autocratic IOs defend autocrats `[clarkdolan_cottiero]`.

**Gap.** The democracy-promoting effect of IOs was estimated in a period of democratic expansion. Whether it holds during global backsliding, with autocratic alternatives available, is untested.

## 12. International order, hierarchy and contestation

**Claimed.** Victors bind themselves in institutions after war `[ikenberry2001]`. Order rests on social purpose `[ruggie1982]`. Hierarchy, not just anarchy, structures world politics `[lake2007]`, `[lake2009book]`, `[lake2010isq]`.

**Settled.** The liberal order is internally contested and its challengers are selective, from China `[weiss2021]`, `[johnston2019]` to populists `[adlernissen2021]`. Hierarchy research has three logics `[mattern2016]`. Unipolar order depends on legitimacy `[finnemore2009]`. Domestic support for liberal internationalism eroded `[kupchan2007]`.

**Contested.** What drives contestation:
- intrusive authority `[borzel2021]`;
- territorial foundations `[simmons2021]`;
- recognition and status `[chia2022]`, `[lin2024]`;
- racial hierarchy `[buzas2021]`;
- network position `[goddard2018]`;
- grievances that push followers toward China `[broz2020china]`.

Chinese visions of order could make China a spoiler, a shirker or a supporter `[schwellerpu2011]`. Whether IR theory should start from regional worlds rather than Western history `[acharya2014]`.

**Gaps.** This is where your order-contestation work sits. The field has many theories of contestation and no common measure of it. Coded UN speeches `[kentikelenisvoeten2021]` and official statements `[lin2024]` show the way. A cross-national, multi-issue measure of states' positions between competing orders, built with validated LLM annotation, would let these theories be tested against each other.

## 13. IR theory, power and the discipline

The theoretical base runs from structural realism `[waltz1979]` through institutionalism `[keohane1984]`, liberalism `[moravcsik1997]` and constructivism `[wendt1999]`. Power has four forms `[barnettduvall2005]`. Network analysis `[hbnetwork2009]`, historical institutionalism `[fioretos2011]` and behavioral IR `[hb2017behavioral]` add newer tools. The discipline's canon excludes women `[owens2018]`, `[maliniak2013]` and non-Western thought `[acharya2014]`.

**Gap.** Paradigm debates faded after 2000, replaced by mid-range theories tested on new data. Whether IR needs a new synthesis for an era of rivalry, interdependence and contestation is an open and much-discussed question.

## 14. Public opinion and foreign policy attitudes

**Settled.** Foreign policy attitudes rest on values `[rathbun2016]` and moral foundations `[kertzer2014moral]`, and form partly bottom-up from peers `[kertzerzeitzoff2017]`. Elite cues matter more on low-information issues `[guisinger2017]`, and reality reasserts itself over time `[baumgroeling2010]`. Leaders respond to opinion `[churecchia2022]`, `[tomz2020io]`. Foreign opinion matters for cooperation `[goldsmith2012]`, and public diplomacy shifts it `[goldsmith2021]`.

**Gap.** Social media changes the elite-press-public chain `[baumpotter2019]`. The effects of state-sponsored foreign media on opinion abroad (see `[mattingly2025]` in the order section) are a fast-growing frontier.

## 15. Climate and environmental cooperation

**Settled.** Democracies make stronger climate commitments but not lower emissions `[battig2009]`. Flexibility raises participation `[vonstein2008]`. Climate is a regime complex `[keohanevictor2011]`, orchestrated through transnational initiatives `[haleroger2014]` that complement national policy `[andonova2017]`. Climate became a governance object through science `[allan2017]`.

**Contested.** Whether climate is a collective-action problem or a distributional conflict over assets `[colgan2021]`. How to build coalitions: compensating vulnerable groups `[gaikwad2022]`, norms of reciprocity `[bechtel2019]`, and correcting misperceived opinion `[mildenberger2019]`.

**Gap.** Climate trade measures (carbon border adjustments) link this map to the IPE map's industrial-policy section. Their effects on cooperation and contestation are unstudied in top journals.

## 16. International courts and adjudication

Legalized dispute resolution varies between interstate and transnational forms `[keohane2000]`. Judges are largely impartial with some national bias `[voeten2008]`, `[pauwelynpelc2023]`. Courts use precedent strategically `[lupuvoeten2012]`, `[pelc2014]`, catalyze legal change beyond the parties `[helfervoeten2014]`, and adjust to override risk `[larssonnaurin2016]`. Legal rulings give leaders political cover `[alleehuth2006]`.

**Gap.** The backlash against international courts (the WTO Appellate Body's collapse, withdrawals from the ICC, attacks on regional human rights courts) needs a theory of when courts survive political attack.

## 17. Diffusion and transnational networks

Liberal policies diffuse through coercion, competition, learning and emulation `[simmonsdobbin2006]`, `[simmons2004]`. Transgovernmental networks drive policy convergence `[bach2010]`, `[newman2008]`. Activism diffuses gender quotas `[hughes2015]`. Dependence networks shape negotiating positions `[goodliffe2009]`.

**Gap.** The diffusion literature studied liberalization. Diffusion of illiberal policies (NGO laws, internet controls, foreign-agent registration) through the same channels is an open, testable question.

## Cross-cutting roadmap: where your work fits

1. **A common measure of order contestation.** Sections 7, 9 and 12 each theorize contestation from a different angle without a shared dependent variable. Validated LLM coding of leader speeches, UN statements and IO documents is the enabling method.
2. **Autocratic institutions as a system.** Sections 4, 6 and 11 each find autocratic counter-institutions (IFIs, regional organizations, rival indicators). Treating them as a connected complex, with the tools of Section 8, connects this map to autocratic rise.
3. **Exit and withdrawal.** Section 9 has new data but few theories linking withdrawal to domestic backlash (the IPE map's Section 3) and to order contestation.
4. **Illiberal diffusion.** Sections 4 and 17 suggest that the channels which spread liberal norms now also spread illiberal policies, which is testable with existing diffusion designs.

## What I could not verify (flagged)

- **Library entries:** missing author names for the Schmidt and Wratil papers were filled in from Crossref.
- **Dates:** Crossref sometimes dates articles by online-first year, so a few show a year earlier than print.
- **Claims:** one-line claims and edge justifications for non-library works are my summaries, not checked against full text.
- **Coverage:** citation counts favor older work and empirical articles, so 2023–2026 work and book-length theory are under-sampled. Regional organizations outside Europe (AU, ASEAN, OAS) are thinly covered.
